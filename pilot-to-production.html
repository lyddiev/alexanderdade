Taking a new glass melting technology from pilot plant to production

Case study · O-I · 2019 to 2020 and 2023 to 2024

The melting technology is proprietary. This page covers the program, the methods, and the people, not how the technology works.

Vendor systems integration, the gates, the people plan, safety, and the knowledge the plants run on.

The problem

A first-of-its-kind glass melting technology had to move from experiments to plants that met a quota and turned a profit. Each site was one of a kind.

The production line was assembled from vendor-built automated systems: robotic batch handling with autonomous guided vehicles (AGV), and AI inspection machines that adjusted the line based on what they saw. Both fed one SCADA control layer. My job was to make them run as one line, and to make sure our engineers could run it without the vendors.

I worked on the pilot plant in 2019 and was brought back in 2023 to run the scale-up.

How it ran

Pilot plant to production: the program, in order

  1. Prove it at the pilot plant.

    I stood up the experimental production site, a full-scale glass plant instrumented with hundreds of sensors and connected devices, to prove the technology for scale-up.

  2. Pick up the scale-up.

    I came back to 12 portfolio teams, 200+ engineers, and three facilities. One production plant was already in flight when I took it on.

  3. Gate every stage.

    Every stage closed with signoffs and demos. Construction and commissioning ran Waterfall, anything software ran Agile, and hardware startup and configuration ran hybrid.

  4. Integrate the vendors.

    Dozens of vendors needed onboarding, data access, and system configuration. I qualified each vendor-built system, validated each control loop into SCADA, ran QA validation, and trained engineers to operate the systems and check the vendor’s work. Testing ran on a segregated copy in a test environment, so production was never touched.

  5. Plan the people and the assets.

    I forecasted engineering headcount, built the capacity model for every asset, and interviewed and hired for the team. Every asset had a BOM, and cost, depreciation, risk, and ROI were reported through SAP and Microsoft Project.

  6. Run the work in one place.

    A templated Azure DevOps intake saved 8 hours a week per person on a 12-person IT team. A ticketing and service management system cut launch time from a month to under a week. A QA validation standard took project timelines from 25 days to 4 and became the organization’s standard.

  7. Leave the knowledge behind.

    I migrated the DITA XML knowledge base to a new platform with Okta SSO for 10,000+ users, live on schedule with adoption up 5x, and rolled out Microsoft Copilot to 300 engineers, cutting onboarding time 35%.
